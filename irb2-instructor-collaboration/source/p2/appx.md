## Appendix H: Instructor interview guide

Semi-structured. Questions follow the topics in the funded plan and are informed by implementation-research interview guides based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (Damschroder et al., 2009) and by studies of faculty drivers and barriers in undergraduate STEM education reform (Shadle, Marker, & Earl, 2017). Interviewers follow up on what the participant raises rather than adhering to the order below.

1. Teaching context: Tell me about the course or courses you teach — your students, class size, format, and what a typical week looks like.

2. Perceived benefits: What benefits, if any, do you see in reflection groups for your students? What about in having students reflect on their use of generative AI?

3. Perceived barriers: What barriers or concerns would you face, or did you face, in integrating these approaches? Which matter most to you?

4. Integration experiences, for those who tried: Walk me through how you integrated the approach into your course. What worked, and what was hard?

5. Deciding not to integrate, where applicable: What led to your decision not to integrate, or to stop? What, if anything, would change that decision?

6. Adaptations: What changes did you make, or would you need to make, to the design for your course, and why?

7. Generative AI in your context: How are you and your department currently handling generative AI in courses? What would make reflection on AI use workable for you?

8. Institutional factors: How do your department and institution view teaching innovation? What incentives, constraints, or expectations shape your decisions about adopting new teaching practices?

9. Support for adoption: What support, materials, or evidence would enable you to adopt or sustain these approaches?

10. Closing: Is there anything else about your context or experiences we should understand?

## Appendix I: Design workshop protocol and consultation procedure

### Design workshops

Workshops are held online, facilitated by the research team, and typically run about one to two hours. Attendees include instructors who integrated a design, attempted to and encountered barriers, or chose not to integrate. A workshop may concern reflection-group designs, integrating generative AI into courses, designing reflection on generative AI use, or a combination.

Ground rules, read at the start of every workshop:

- Participation is voluntary, and you may leave at any time.
- What is shared here is confidential — please do not share others' contributions outside the workshop.
- The research team reports findings only in de-identified form, and does not share your contributions or your adoption decisions with your institution, department, or colleagues.
- Attending this workshop does not obligate you to use any of these designs in your course.
- Recording occurs only if everyone present agrees, and anyone may ask that recording be paused at any point.

The facilitator opens by stating the purpose of the session, then attendees work through the topics they came for: what they have tried and how it went, what makes adoption hard in their context, and what designs or adaptations would work for their courses. The team provides its current materials as a starting point. Beyond the ground rules and the session's stated purpose, attendees direct how the session's time is used, and the facilitator follows the group.

Data collected: facilitator and attendee notes, the materials attendees produce during the session, and recordings only where everyone present agreed.

### Consultation ("office hours") procedure

Consultations are by appointment, are not recorded, and last as long as the instructor finds useful. The PI takes notes, which may be handwritten and are transcribed by the named researchers.

Confirmation, given at the start of each session with a consenting instructor:

> "Before we start: as we discussed in the consent form, I'd like to take notes on our conversation for our research on how instructors adopt these approaches. I'm not recording — these are just my notes. They're confidential and reported only in de-identified form, and you can tell me at any time, today or later, to leave something out — a part of this conversation or the whole session. Is that OK for today?"

Instructors who have not consented to the research receive the same consultation support, and no notes are taken for research.

## Appendix O: Reflection group meetings

Each reflection group meets for a short session at a regular interval — for example, weekly — over the course of a term. Each meeting has three parts, drawn from the team's prior reflection-group work (Nelson et al., 2025): members share progress and challenges; they affirm challenges, encourage one another, and celebrate accomplishments; and they generate ideas for improvement.

Before the first meeting, members complete an icebreaker activity adapted from an experimental-psychology activity for creating interpersonal closeness (Aron et al., 1997), with questions that might evoke trauma removed and question durations shortened for the group setting. Members are told they may pass on any question.

> {your input needed here — attach the team's full adapted icebreaker question list.}

Groups are provided guided questions as a starting point for their meetings: what went well for your learning; what supported it; what might be improved; possible improvement goals for the coming week; and which goal you are choosing and why. **Once a group has started meeting, its members decide how to use their time.** The guided questions are a starting point, and groups may change them or add to them. Topic modules — a meeting's agenda focused on a particular professional skill, including two to three modules on critical generative AI use — may be used as the design develops.

After each meeting, members submit individual written answers to that meeting's questions and choose an improvement goal. These individual responses are viewable by the research team for consenting students, and by the course under its normal assignment handling.

Adaptations agreed with a collaborating instructor — for example meeting length, cadence, or which topic modules are used — are documented in the study file and available on request. Responses to the guided questions and to any topic-module questions are collected and analyzed under this protocol for consenting students. Any additional questions an instructor adds for their own course purposes are the instructor's own course materials.
