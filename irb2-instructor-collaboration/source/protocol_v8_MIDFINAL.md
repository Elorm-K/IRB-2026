## 2. Summary

This study investigates how instructors adopt, adapt, and sustain two related teaching approaches, and what effects those approaches have on their students. The two approaches are structured peer reflection groups — small groups of students who meet briefly and regularly with a guided agenda to reflect on their learning — and reflection on students' own use of generative AI.

The research team's prior work found that structured peer reflection groups in a course supported professional skills including iterative improvement, psychological safety, and communication, with effects students reported carrying beyond the course (Nelson et al., 2025), and that a comparative reflection assignment on programming with and without generative AI supported learning about critical AI use (Fernandez & Nelson, 2026). Approaches like these only matter at scale if instructors beyond the original team can adopt them. Implementation research shows adoption depends on understanding adopters' contexts, barriers, and adaptations (Bauer & Kirchner, 2020; Shadle, Marker, & Earl, 2017), and prior research on reflection and peer support explains why the approaches themselves are expected to help students (Chan & Lee, 2021; Lim et al., 2022).

This is a mixed-methods study. The qualitative components are the design workshops, consultation notes, interviews, and written reflections; the quantitative components are the pre-course, post-course, and longitudinal surveys.

Following the funded CAREER project's plan, this protocol therefore treats design itself as research. Design workshops with instructors, surveys and interviews about barriers and benefits, and consultation notes are collected and analyzed to understand what makes these approaches adoptable. Workshops may concern reflection groups, designing generative AI integration in courses, or designing reflection on generative AI use.

Two populations take part: (a) instructors, faculty, and other educators at the University of Maine and at other institutions; and (b) students in collaborating instructors' courses where these approaches, or comparison surveys, are used. The two populations are studied together because the research question is precisely how an instructor's adaptations play out in their own students' outcomes, which cannot be answered by studying either group alone.

Because the research team develops and advocates the designs being adopted, this protocol separates collaboration from research participation at every step. The three separable choices — working with the team, using a design in a course, and allowing research use of data — and the protections attached to each are set out in Section 4 under Coercion considerations.

For the purposes of this research, "adoption outcomes" refers to instructors' decisions to use, adapt, sustain, or not use these designs; the adaptations they make; the barriers and enablers they encounter; and their perceptions of the benefits and costs. "Student outcomes" refers to professional skills and dispositions (such as iterative improvement, lifelong learning, psychological safety, and communication), self-efficacy, professional identity, critical and reflective use of generative AI, and self-reported learning and career outcomes.

Research Questions:

1. What barriers and enablers shape whether instructors adopt, adapt, and sustain reflection-group pedagogies and reflection on generative AI use in their courses and institutions?

2. How do designs developed together with instructors differ across courses and institutional contexts, and what do those differences suggest about designing for adoption?

3. What adoption outcomes follow from participation in design workshops and consultations?

4. What are the effects of these approaches on student outcomes in courses beyond the research team's own, including at other institutions?

Methods:

This research is expected to run over multiple years under the NSF CAREER grant, with an initial round of instructor design workshops anticipated in fall 2026, classroom use in collaborating instructors' courses beginning in a subsequent term, and a yearly faculty adoption design cycle thereafter. Consent precedes every activity below; see Section 5.

For instructor participants:

1. Interest and barriers survey. Instructors are invited to a confidential survey about their teaching context, their prior use of reflection and AI-reflection activities, their interest in these approaches, the benefits and barriers they perceive, and the support that would make integration feasible, with an option to volunteer for a follow-up interview. The survey takes approximately 10–15 minutes.

2. Optional interviews. Consenting instructors take part in confidential interviews about their teaching context, perceived barriers and benefits, and — for those who tried the approaches — their implementation experiences. {{Interviews are semi-structured and cover teaching context, perceived benefits and barriers, integration experiences or reasons for deciding against integration, adaptations made, institutional incentives and constraints, and what support would enable adoption; the interviewer follows up on what the participant raises. For example, an interviewer might ask "walk me through how you integrated the approach into your course — what worked, and what was hard?" or "what support, materials, or evidence would let you sustain this?" The topics follow the funded plan and are informed by implementation-research interview guides (Damschroder et al., 2009; Shadle, Marker, & Earl, 2017).}} Interviews take approximately 30–60 minutes, are conducted on Zoom or in person based on the participant's preference, and are audio-recorded; no video is recorded. Participants must agree to the recording, or written notes are taken instead. {{Interviews are covered by the instructor consent form, Appendix A.}}

3. Design workshops. Instructors are invited to design workshops to shape (a) reflection-group designs, (b) the integration of generative AI into their courses, and/or (c) designs for reflection on generative AI use — including for their own courses and institutions, identifying challenges and barriers to adoption, and proposing designs and improvements. Workshops invite instructors who {{have integrated a design, who attempted to and encountered barriers, or who chose not to integrate}}. Workshop notes and the materials attendees produce are analyzed qualitatively. Workshops are held online and typically run about one to two hours. An initial round is anticipated in fall 2026, repeated each design cycle, and also including roughly yearly adoption-cycle workshops. The workshop protocol and ground rules are in Appendix E.

4. Consultation ("office hours") notes. The PI holds by-appointment consultations for interested instructors. For instructors who consent, the PI takes notes and analyzes them as qualitative research on designing for adoption. Consultations are not recorded; notes may be handwritten and are transcribed by the named researchers. Note-taking for research is confirmed at the start of each session, and an instructor may ask that any part of a session, or a whole session, be left out of the research record, at the time or later. The consultation procedure is in Appendix E.

5. Longitudinal follow-up. Consenting instructors receive brief follow-up surveys in later semesters, about their adoption decisions, the adaptations they made, what barriers or supports mattered, and whether they plan to continue, and/or invitations to a follow-up interview. Each follow-up survey takes approximately 10–15 minutes.

6. Faculty adaptation. Instructors who adapt a design for their own course evaluation are encouraged to use the short pre- and post-course measures in Appendix H so different versions can be compared. Where an instructor wishes their students' data to be part of this research, their students are consented under items 9–16 below.

For students in collaborating instructors' courses:

7. Classroom use. Collaborating instructors integrate reflection groups, reflection-on-AI-use activities, or both into their courses as a planned part of the course, following the team's materials as adapted with the instructor. The course activity occurs whether or not students consent to the research, and is not itself research.

8. Group meetings and individual reflections. Each group holds a roughly 30-minute online meeting at a regular interval (for example, weekly), in which:

- participants discuss what is going well in their learning and what is supporting their learning;
- participants give updates on their goal from the previous week, where applicable;
- participants identify areas for potential improvement in their learning; and
- each participant chooses one thing to improve for the next week.

{{Before the first meeting, members complete an icebreaker activity adapted from an experimental-psychology activity for creating interpersonal closeness (Aron et al., 1997), with questions that might evoke trauma removed and question durations shortened for the group setting; members are told they may pass on any question.}} After each meeting, members submit individual written answers to that meeting's questions and choose an improvement goal. Once a group has started meeting, its members decide how to use their time; the questions above are a starting point, and groups may change or add to them. {{Topic modules — a meeting's agenda focused on a particular professional skill, including two to three modules on critical generative AI use — may be used as the design develops. Adaptations agreed with a collaborating instructor, such as meeting length, cadence, or which topic modules are used, are documented in the study file and available on request.}} Reflection group meetings are not recorded.

9. Student consent. Before any student data are collected, a member of the research team presents the study using the script in Appendix G and distributes the consent form (Appendix B). Participation has no effect on assignments, activities, or grades. Students opt out by emailing a member of the research team who is not the instructor of their course.

10. Student surveys. Consenting students complete pre- and post-course surveys with the student outcome measures (Appendix H). Surveys are confidential, take approximately 10–15 minutes each, and are open for roughly two weeks at the start and end of the course. Students in courses that use reflection groups also complete a short group-matching survey (approximately 5 minutes), also in Appendix H, which is used to place them in a group with compatible scheduling.

11. Student reflection materials. With consent, students' reflection-group materials — their group's meeting notes and their individual written reflections, including responses to any topic modules, as described in item 8 — are analyzed by the research team. No grades, academic records, or other student-records data are collected.

12. Longitudinal follow-up. Consenting students are invited to confidential follow-up surveys roughly every six months after the course, for up to four years (up to 8 surveys total), using the survey consent form in Appendix C, with survey content in Appendix I.

13. Student and alumni interviews. A purposeful subset of students and former students is invited to confidential interviews about their experiences, the professional skills they use, and changes over time. Interviews take approximately 30–60 minutes, are conducted online or in person, and are audio-recorded; no video is recorded, and written notes are taken instead if the participant prefers. The consent form is Appendix D.

14. Student design input and group remedies. Students may be invited to a design session held during a class session, to an end-of-semester retrospective about what should change, and to a brief optional mid-course feedback survey so the team can adjust during the term. Notes and materials from these are analyzed qualitatively. Groups do not always work well: a student in a group that has stopped functioning may join a different group, which opts in to adding a member, or form a new group.

15. Comparison courses. Where a collaborating instructor's institution offers a similar course without these approaches, students in that course may be recruited for surveys only, as a comparison group, and are also invited to the longitudinal follow-up surveys. Because no course activity is involved, consent is obtained through the survey consent form, using the comparison-course adaptation in Appendix C. The recruitment script is adapted accordingly (Appendix G).

16. External sites. Before any activity in a course at another institution, the team obtains site approval — a letter of support from the appropriate official at that institution, requested per Appendix J — and completes whatever human-subjects review arrangement that institution requires, which may be a reliance agreement with the University of Maine, an exempt or not-research determination, or local review by that institution's IRB. Documentation of the arrangement is provided to the UMaine IRB before any research activity begins at that site.

An instructor may withdraw their course from the study at any time. Data already collected from consenting students are retained per Section 6, but no new data are collected from that course. If an instructor withdraws partway through a term, the reflection groups continue as a normal part of the course, since they are part of the course rather than part of the research, and students are told that no further data will be collected.

Data collected:

1.1 Instructor survey responses: teaching context, interest, perceived barriers and benefits, and email address; and instructor follow-up survey responses in later semesters (items 1 and 5 above).

1.2 Instructor interview recordings and transcripts, or written notes where an instructor prefers not to be recorded (item 2 above).

1.3 Design workshop notes, the materials attendees produce, and recordings where everyone present agreed (Appendix E).

1.4 Student group-matching survey responses (Appendix H).

1.5 Student reflection-group materials: group meeting notes and individual written reflections, including responses to topic modules (item 8 above).

1.6 Student pre- and post-course survey responses: demographics, email address, and student outcome measures (Appendix H).

1.7 Student longitudinal follow-up survey responses (Appendix I).

1.8 Student design-session, retrospective, and mid-course feedback notes, materials, and survey responses.

1.9 Student and alumni interview recordings and transcripts, and researcher notes on work examples described or shown (Appendix D).

## 4. Participant recruitment

Participant Population:

**Instructors.** Faculty, lecturers, and other educators who teach, or are interested in teaching with, reflection-group pedagogies or reflection on generative AI use. Across several yearly design cycles over the protocol's duration we may have up to approximately 200 instructors, including instructors who take part in one activity only.

**Students.** Students enrolled in collaborating instructors' courses where these approaches, or comparison surveys, are used. Class sizes are typically 30–50 students and the team anticipates collaborating with roughly 10 courses per year once the design has stabilized, so over the protocol's duration we may have up to approximately 1,000 students, including students in comparison courses who take surveys only.

All participants are aged 18 or older. Individuals younger than 18 are excluded; where a participating course may enroll students under 18, the recruitment script and consent form state the age requirement and those students are not enrolled in the research, while still taking part in the course activity as usual.

Participant Identification and Recruitment Procedures:

Instructors are identified and invited through mailing lists, targeted email invitations to instructors whose public course listings indicate relevant teaching, professional networks and conferences, and existing collaborators (letters of collaboration accompany the funded grant). The team will also recruit more broadly through public announcements, a project website, and social media. Recruitment texts are in Appendix F.

Students are recruited within collaborating courses using the script in Appendix G, delivered so that consent is never administered by the host instructor: a member of the research team presents the study in person or by recorded announcement, distributes the consent form, and receives any opt-outs. Where a course is taught by a member of the research team, that course falls under the team's approved course-based protocol and is not enrolled here.

Coercion considerations: the research team holds no supervisory authority over participating instructors, and does not evaluate them, employ them, or make decisions about their advancement. However, because collaboration may carry perceived professional stakes, and because the research team develops the designs instructors are asked to evaluate, the protocol adds the following protections. Research consent is separated from collaboration. Attending a workshop does not obligate an instructor to use any design in their course, and an instructor may use the materials without their course or their students taking part in research. Individual adoption decisions — including declining — are confidential and reported only in de-identified form.

For students, the course activity happens whether or not they take part in the research; the research team, never the host instructor, presents the study and administers consent; the host instructor does not know who participated until after final grades are submitted; and participation has no effect on grades. If a member of the research team is in a supervisory or evaluative relationship with a participating instructor, that team member does not recruit, consent, interview, or analyze identifiable data for that instructor or their students.

## 5. Informed consent

Informed consent is obtained electronically, and consent precedes any research activity.

There are {{four}} consent forms, because there are {{four}} genuinely distinct activity-and-population combinations:

- Appendix A — instructors, for the survey, workshops, consultation notes, {{interviews,}} and follow-up surveys. Signed before any research activity, including before the interest and barriers survey and before any workshop from which data are analyzed.
- Appendix B — students in collaborating courses, distributed in class by a member of the research team and signed before any student data are collected.
- Appendix C — student longitudinal follow-up surveys, and all surveys taken by comparison-course students. The form appears as the first page of each survey and covers consent for the study; submission of the survey indicates consent.
- Appendix D — student and alumni interviews, signed before the interview begins.

## 6. Confidentiality

Participants in reflection groups and design workshops will be asked to respect the confidentiality of their peers and not share personal information discussed in the sessions. However, we cannot guarantee complete confidentiality in a group setting. Participants will be informed of this limitation in the consent process.

Individual instructors' adoption decisions, workshop contributions, and consultation content are confidential: they are reported only in de-identified form, and are not shared with the instructor's department, institution, or colleagues. Students' gender and disability status, where reported on the group-matching survey, are never disclosed to other students, to the host instructor, or to teaching assistants.

Initially, all data will be stored on a secure cloud storage platform {{(Google Drive)}} that encrypts all data at rest and in transit with AES-256 bit encryption and is password protected. Survey data — the instructor surveys, the student pre-, post-, and longitudinal surveys, and the group-matching survey — will be collected and stored in a secured cloud platform {{(Qualtrics)}}. IP addresses are not collected in any survey. Interviews are conducted online {{(Zoom)}} or in person; where an interview or workshop is recorded, recordings are removed {{from Zoom}} within 72 hours and stored on that secure cloud storage platform, and recordings made on a handheld recorder for in-person interviews are removed from the device within 72 hours. Recordings are transcribed by the named researchers; no outside transcription service is used. Handwritten consultation notes are transcribed by the named researchers and the originals destroyed once transcription is complete. Reflection group meetings are not recorded.

Within 14 days after the end of a course, or for data collected outside a course or after a course, within 14 days of collection, all data will be archived to a separate archival folder on the secure cloud storage platform, with all participant identifiers (such as email addresses) replaced with a randomly generated encrypted key. During this archiving, all data from individuals not consenting to the research will be filtered out and deleted, by looking up their email addresses and removing the records associated with them. The key file that maps to participants' email addresses and participant demographic data will be stored in a separate folder on the secure cloud storage platform; it is stored electronically and is encrypted. Five years after a participant's participation ends, their email address will be deleted from the key file; for example, for a student in a Fall 2026 course, the key entry will be deleted by the end of December 2031. This is to allow participants to withdraw their consent later if they wish. We can only delete participant data while the key exists.

In summary, the following data from participants who take part in the research will be retained indefinitely in de-identified form: instructor survey and follow-up survey responses (1.1), instructor interview transcripts and notes (1.2), design workshop notes and materials (1.3), student group-matching survey responses (1.4), student reflection-group materials (1.5), student pre- and post-course survey responses (1.6), student longitudinal follow-up survey responses (1.7), student design-session and feedback records (1.8), and student and alumni interview transcripts and notes (1.9). {{Original audio recordings of interviews and workshops are deleted at the same time as the participant's email address is deleted from the key file, five years after their participation ends; for example, for participation ending in Fall 2026, by the end of December 2031.}}

De-identified quotations from survey responses, written reflections, interview transcripts, workshop notes, and consultation notes may be used in publications and presentations, with no participant, course, or institution identified. De-identified data may be shared with collaborating researchers outside the research team for analysis, and may be deposited in a public research repository to meet the funder's public-access requirements. Original recordings are never shared or published. Identifiable data are accessible only to the research personnel named in Section 3 and to additional trained personnel added under the PI's supervision.

Participants' privacy and right to withdraw from the study will be respected, so we will delete any of their data upon request as long as the request is within 5 years of the end of their participation, as otherwise their email address will have been deleted from the key file.

## 7. Risks to participants

There are risks of time and inconvenience to participants. There is a minimal risk of a data breach; we expect a potential risk of embarrassment in that event. This is mitigated by the de-identification, storage, and retention procedures described in Section 6.

For instructors, there is a potential professional-reputation risk if their adoption struggles, their critical opinions, or their decision not to adopt became known. This is mitigated by the confidentiality protections in Section 6, by the workshop ground rules, and by the separation of collaboration from research participation described in Section 4.

For students, because their instructor collaborates with the research team, there could be perceived pressure to participate. This is mitigated by the deployment protections in Section 4. Information a student shares in a group discussion could also be repeated by another group member; group members are asked to respect each other's confidentiality, but it cannot be guaranteed.

There is also a risk of participants' data leaking from third-party applications they may use. Participants will likely already use many third-party applications which carry these risks (Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, and so on). While using tools as part of the study — and as part of critical instruction in generative AI — we will emphasize to participants that they should not share or submit content to third-party services that might be used contrary to the learners' interests.

The surveys and interviews will make clear in the introduction that any question can be skipped if the participant does not feel comfortable answering.

## 8. Benefits

There are no direct benefits to participants, but participants may learn and reflect critically about their learning, professional skills, self-efficacy, and professional identity over time, which may help them develop skills useful in their education and careers, and they may benefit from community, peer support, and professional networks formed through the groups.

The overall benefits of the research include improving understanding of scalable, adoptable, and effective reflection-based practices for supporting learning, professional skills, and lifelong learning across educational and professional settings. Findings may inform pedagogical practice, support adoption across institutions, and contribute to understanding how to sustain professional growth after graduation.

## Appendix E: Design workshop protocol and consultation procedure

### Design workshops

Workshops are held online, facilitated by the research team, and typically run about one to two hours. Attendees include instructors who integrated a design, attempted to and encountered barriers, or chose not to integrate. A workshop may concern reflection-group designs, integrating generative AI into courses, designing reflection on generative AI use, or a combination.

Ground rules, read at the start of every workshop:

- Participation is voluntary, and you may leave at any time.
- What is shared here is confidential — please do not share others' contributions outside the workshop.
- The research team reports findings only in de-identified form, and does not share your contributions or your adoption decisions with your institution, department, or colleagues.
- Attending this workshop does not obligate you to use any of these designs in your course.
- Recording occurs only if everyone present agrees, and anyone may ask that recording be paused at any point.

The facilitator opens by stating the purpose of the session, then attendees work through the topics they came for: what they have tried and how it went, what makes adoption hard in their context, and what designs or adaptations would work for their courses. The team provides its current materials as a starting point. Beyond the ground rules and the session's stated purpose, attendees direct how the session's time is used, and the facilitator follows the group.

Data collected: facilitator and attendee notes, the materials attendees produce during the session, and recordings only where everyone present agreed.

### Consultation ("office hours") procedure

Consultations are by appointment, are not recorded, and last as long as the instructor finds useful. The PI takes notes, which may be handwritten and are transcribed by the named researchers.

Confirmation, given at the start of each session with a consenting instructor:

> "Before we start: as we discussed in the consent form, I'd like to take notes on our conversation for our research on how instructors adopt these approaches. I'm not recording — these are just my notes. They're confidential and reported only in de-identified form, and you can tell me at any time, today or later, to leave something out — a part of this conversation or the whole session. Is that OK for today?"

Instructors who have not consented to the research receive the same consultation support, and no notes are taken for research.
